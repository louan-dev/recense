# Recense

One system in three states, held together less by any single piece than by the relationship running between
them: the strip on your Mac, the library in your cloud, and the sync that keeps the two honest with each other.
The network earns an occasional role, improving things on the days it shows up, and offline stays the baseline
everything else actually runs on.

Underneath the three states sits one relationship worth being precise about, which is that a thought behaves like
one piece of matter, and treating it as a file is where most other tools quietly go wrong. Start a sentence by
hand on an iPad, carry it to a Mac keyboard, finish it by voice in a car, and so Recense treats all three as one
page moving through three states rather than three documents quietly drifting apart from each other. Anything
Recense adds along the way, a transcript, a translation, a summary, keeps a traceable edge back to exactly what
it came from. That's provenance, and it keeps the system honest about the difference between what you actually
said and what a model merely guessed on your behalf.

This began somewhere other than a metaphor I went looking for. I'm drawn to materials and the relationships
running between them, and following that draw far enough turns up a genuine anomaly sitting right inside
written expression: ink on a page, a byte on a disk, a pixel lighting up, each one settles the matter question
on its own, immediately. The real question skips past whether a thought behaves like matter once it's written
down, since it already is matter, and lands instead on how much of its so-called transformation has ever
actually been allowed to behave the way matter does. Water moves between solid, liquid, gas and plasma, and the
same atoms carry through every one of those changes, rearranged but kept whole, running just as cleanly backward
as forward.

Coming at this from the material end rather than the software end makes it a strange thing to stumble into, and
a genuinely promising one, because those same transformations are already running inside the person doing the
writing. Water changing state is part of what keeps a body alive at all, every exchange reversible, every step
conserving what it started with. A thought forms, comes apart, reassembles into something clearer, and arrives
at that shape on its own. A writing surface built to honor that would line up with forces already at work
inside whoever uses it, instead of asking them to hold still.

Writing entered the world under a constraint of pure physics. Whoever first pressed ink into a surface had one
instrument, one surface, one direction, and the traditions built on that inheritance kept its stillness long
after the constraint itself lifted. History has treated the act as sedentary ever since, a body held in place
while something finished arrives on a page. Most of a thought lives in the motion though, in the transition
rather than the arrival, and action comes out of movement instead of preceding it. Move, and the thing worth
saying surfaces. Which sense carries the language draws the boundary of what can surface through it, so a
thought spoken finds shapes a thought typed leaves alone, and a thought drawn finds others again, each sense
lending its own temperature and color to a stimulus that stays identical underneath. Writing is a marriage
between the transience of the senses and the transient states of matter, and the instruments to honor it
arrived centuries after the tradition had already hardened around their absence.

That hardened tradition comes with a small set of terms to agree to before a single word arrives, and the terms
are stranger than they first look. Pick a phase now and hold it for the duration. Will this be an `.odt` file, an
`.md` file, a plain text file, paper, or a recording? Commit, because the symphony coming out of a mind stays
one continuous piece on one condition, that it lands on one medium the whole way through. Remain ice, the terms
say, so that everything making the senses dance holds a single shape and a single format from beginning to end.
What the agreement costs arrives later, as a dampened resonance. Two sections kept near each other hold a
charge, and even the friction between them, suspended five semitones apart, loses its old insistence once they
are allowed to sound at the same time. Dissonance keeps its temperament and turns generous, wide enough to
cradle a muse through the crossing. Filed apart, each section survives intact. Only the interval between them
goes missing, and the interval was the music.

The entropy inside a person keeps moving regardless, so the arrival of one particularly heartfelt memory,
something warm surfacing with its own weather, sublimates the writer straight from ice to vapor while the file
sits there holding out for the ice it was promised. Meanwhile the thing generating all of it runs on precisely
the fluidity the software declines to pass along. Neural activity shifting states continuously, the movements a
body makes in answer to being alive, the smaller movements
happening further in that make iteration possible at all, every one of them fluid, every one of them
reversible, and together they amount to the actual gift that lets a person imprint any form of their
consciousness onto anything whatsoever. Every sentence coming out of that process is the same matter
rearranged, semantically and idiosyncratically, the way the same atoms rearrange moving between ice and vapor.
The software takes all of this in and asks, politely, for a format commitment up front. A tool at its best
becomes an extension of the person holding it, the way an instrument stops being an object somewhere around the
third hour of playing it. These particular tools tend to run the other direction, setting a little more distance
between a person and their own thinking with every format they ask them to choose between.

The principle holds in both directions. Move, and the action follows. Move, and the thought and the mood follow
with it, more readily the more degrees of movement stay available. That points at the shape of an answer, a page
existing in several states at once rather than one at a time. The same document standing as a `.docx` and an
`.odt`, an annotated `.pdf`, a dictated passage, an `.md` file, pencil and annotation the way a slate takes
them, speech marked up and audio made searchable, each one a state of the same matter rather than a separate
artifact. A page like that holds a symphony of the senses together adaptively, carrying the thought and the mood
that came out of the movement in the first place. Writer's block loosens its grip once movement and writing stop
sitting in opposition, and the generative potential inside that motion, the kind nature runs through every state
of matter continuously, reaches the person writing instead of waiting on the far side of a format decision. Code
that marries those two ideas lets the act of writing run on the same physics as the person doing it.

Nature settled this problem long ago, and so the design borrows the solution whole rather than inventing a
rival. Matter keeps one identity through every transformation it undergoes, solid to liquid to gas to plasma
and back again, and what survives each crossing is the matter itself, five states deep and still the same
substance. A document can be built on that premise, as a single unchanging vessel holding every phase
transformation available to writing at the same time, all of them present, each one reachable from any other.
Of all the practices a person inherits, writing sits furthest from behaving that way while standing closest to
the chance of it, since what passes across a page is already thought caught mid-transition. Expression asks for
vulnerability to work at all, and vulnerability renews itself in the spending, a currency that grows through use
and a portal that opens again each time somebody is willing to walk through it. Software holding every state of
a page at once lowers the toll on that willingness, and so whatever waits on the other side of it becomes
reachable on an ordinary afternoon.

Sit for a moment with what that freedom would actually feel like. A thought could build the way a phrase builds
on an instrument, pressure and release, a figure tried one way and immediately tried another, fluid enough to
double back and come out stronger for the detour. Anyone who has improvised for even an hour knows that feeling
already, a line finding itself through revision that happens live rather than afterward. Writing has stayed
outside that for a long while. The part worth wondering about is how many more people would write, and write
far more of it, once composing stopped asking them to settle on a shape before they knew what they meant.

What's stranger is that the proof of another way already sits inside every single keystroke. Fixing a typo,
deleting a sentence, rephrasing a thought until it means something closer to what was intended, these already
count as forward and reversible transformations of the exact same matter, the same principle water already
follows finding its way back from vapor to liquid. Software already knows how to do this. It does it every time
someone hits backspace. It just quietly refuses to carry that same principle past the edge of one keystroke, one
app, one device, which is exactly where the whole thing turns rigid again, and rejecting how matter actually
transforms right at that boundary is the real non-fluidity worth naming here, a genuinely interesting paradigm
worth sitting inside of rather than resolving too quickly. Part of why that boundary holds is structural rather
than purely technical: software tends to build vertically instead, deeper into its own silo, more features
stacked on the same foundation, because a market built on walls rather than bridges rewards depth within a fence
over width across one. Write three paragraphs today across three different file formats, and watch the actual
cost of that arrangement show up: those paragraphs usually end up scattered across three separate files, three
towers instead of one continuous, horizontal page, each one needing its own key to get back in. Recense's actual bet runs the other way: one page, one piece of matter, free
to change its state in either direction while keeping every atom of what it started as.

Isn't it interesting, though, how overlooked this particular opportunity is. The toolkit to actually close the
gap, a touchscreen, a sync engine, dictation, has existed for a while now, and most of what gets built with it
still reaches for the next feature rather than actual continuity of thought. A report started on a phone in one
app, continued on a laptop in another, finished by voice in a third, still usually ends up as a `.note` file, an
`.odt` file and a `.md` file rather than one document carrying one thought through all three, and the honest
reason turns out almost charmingly simple: competition in a free market rewards exactly that fragmentation,
since a company that locks a format to its own software keeps a customer it might otherwise lose to a better
one. Picture the other version for a moment: genuine bridging across formats and platforms, actually aligned
with how matter behaves and how a thought already moves through different postures and devices. Imagine what
the world's art and literature would start to look like once thinking and writing moved as freely between
states as a phase transformation does, flexible rather than binary, a thought allowed to be liquid one minute
and solid the next while keeping everything of itself intact in between. Imagine the insights that might surface
once writing like that became genuinely available to anyone, regardless of device, language, a body that works
differently, or a connection that happens to be down. That alone feels like the more interesting economy to
build toward, one where wider exposure to more platforms happens almost automatically, and retention comes from
being worth staying for rather than from the cost of leaving.

Every cost here earns its keep, which I mean as the principle behind the whole
build. Recense runs as one process rather than a handful of them, and the same app serves three languages and
three devices instead of a separate build for each, because keeping one boring path working costs less over the
long run than keeping three clever ones alive at once, and so the slow, expensive calls, transcription, a
rewrite, a translation, run once, at rest, cached by exactly what they were asked to do rather than live on every
keystroke. The payoff stays invisible today. It compounds the way boring investments do, where a cycle you save
this year is a cycle you're still saving in five, and the reason behind it stays quietly unnecessary to remember.

The longer essay, on improvisation, the measure of a gift, a score for speech and a shared tuning for the world's
ideas, is [the preface](docs/recense-preface.md). It's the why, and everything past this point is what actually
happened once I tried building that why into something that runs.

## What works offline
Recense itself runs on the Mac, start to finish: transcription, the light, loops, speed, sound and Draftpad all
happen there. Gallery's apps keep whatever they've already cached, and share links, the web app and CalDAV are
the parts that openly admit they need the internet, failing plainly rather than failing silently once it's gone.

Transcription is actually two engines sharing one interface, and that pairing is itself a relationship I'd like
to understand better than I currently do: Apple's own on-device SpeechAnalyzer, wherever macOS or iOS already
speaks the language, sits above a self-hosted model, Omnilingual ASR, for whatever Apple's coverage has yet to
reach. Depending on Apple's engine means depending on Apple's own release schedule for new languages, a
dependency that sits outside Recense's own control. Depending on the self-hosted model underneath it means
depending on Recense's own ability to keep maintaining a model that stays entirely Recense's responsibility,
since Apple's own roadmap points elsewhere. Both dependencies stand on their own, and so the seam between the
two engines is still supposed to vanish regardless. Whether it actually does, and what it would take for Apple's
own reach to eventually make the second engine unnecessary, is one of the boundaries mapped out below.

Carrying files between the Mac, iPhone and iPad needs no internet either (FEAT-09). Its rules are built:
`shared/carrysync.swift` turns each scan of the homes into an append-only log of put, move, trash and restore
(there is no delete), and settles two devices' logs without ever overwriting a file. `shared/carrypair.swift`
pairs two devices once by comparing six digits on both screens, as Bluetooth does. `shared/p2ptransport.swift` is the
radio link itself (FEAT-02, MultipeerConnectivity): finds the other device over Bluetooth or peer-to-peer Wi-Fi, no
router, no internet, and carries both the pairing exchange and the carry log. Pairing and settling are tested without
radios; the link itself is proven on real devices, airplane mode on. Check: `swiftc -O shared/notesync.swift shared/carrysync.swift mobile/ios/Tests/carrysync/main.swift -o "$TMPDIR/carrysync" && "$TMPDIR/carrysync"`.
The glue is wired (`shared/carrymanager.swift`, `shared/carryapply.swift`, a "Devices" screen on the Mac and on
iOS): pairing works end to end, and "Carry Now" settles for real — each device keeps its own append-only ops
log, two paired devices exchange what the other lacks, and a move, a trash or a restore replays correctly on
the other side, never overwriting a file. The one part still open: a real conflict always keeps both copies
rather than merging, because no base is tracked yet for the text merge `shared/notesync.swift` already knows
how to do. Pairing also works from a keyboard
alone: `recense carry pair` in the Mac's Terminal (reachable from the iPhone or iPad over SSH, Library › Terminal)
prints the same six-digit code and waits for Enter where the GUI waits for a tap on "They Match". iOS and iPadOS have no terminal
of their own, so pairing there also runs by voice or Shortcuts (`mobile/HostApp/iOS/PairIntent.swift`): "Pair a
device" reads the code back, "Confirm pairing" is the spoken "They Match".

Cloudflare serves purely as storage and caching, deliberately swappable: the plan is a one-command move onto a
Mac mini, on the theory that a signature, a subscription or a vendor's roadmap expiring should leave every bit of
this still working.

## What keeps running when the grid fails
Offline-first grew here less as a design preference than as a belief formed by lived necessity. A year spent
away from home wifi, prepaid data abroad that ran out mid-week and stayed empty, a flight, a boat, a stretch of
countryside holding zero signal. Every one of these counted as the ordinary condition writing had to survive,
long before any cloud ever caught up with it, lived rather than imagined at a whiteboard. Most software treats
the network as usually present and occasionally interrupted, and I'd rather design for the opposite assumption,
where the network is usually absent, and so every moment it does show up counts as a brief, welcome exception
that catches things up and then steps out of the way again.

The remote end of this carries a second, quieter purpose too. Writer's block famously lifts out in the
countryside, at sea, somewhere in a national park, anywhere a network and a research framework both go quiet at
once, which counts as a real and repeatable effect worth taking seriously rather than treating as coincidence. A
good idea rarely substantiates itself purely out of one person's own experience. It tends to come from many
sources at once: truths and contradictions and beliefs that hold at the same time, voices answering each other
across languages, perspectives that complement each other in ways a single mind sitting alone would rarely
produce by itself. A remote place with its connection cut entirely turns into its own kind of trouble once the
isolation runs on long enough, since a self-limiting thought goes uncalled, an unbalanced one goes uncorrected,
and creative output that only ever substantiates itself against personal experience eventually runs thin on
material. The more useful answer carries research and the rest of the world's narratives along instead, in a
form that works offline and catches up whenever a connection appears, present in the remote place rather than
replacing it, sensing the surroundings as closely as a tool that stays online around the clock would, rather than
asking a person to choose between the two.

The failure modes worth designing against stay fairly ordinary once you've actually lived inside a few of them.
Depending on the electrical grid, depending on an internet provider, depending on a device's own battery and
depending on a single company's software for the physical act of writing are four separate dependencies that
rarely get named together as one set, which is itself the principle worth naming here: they stack on top of each
other constantly, and a failure in any one of them tends to expose how little the others were actually
protecting you. They loop back on each other too, in a way a feature checklist tends to miss entirely: the grid
failing leaves the battery as the only thing still standing, the battery draining makes a live connection the
thing that matters most for reaching anyone, and losing that connection is exactly the moment a vendor's cloud
stops answering as well. The four of them take turns being the one thing holding the line, which is the circular
shape a systems view catches on sight. A single company's outage taking down the one writing tool an entire profession got quietly
funneled into, carrying only a thin fallback at best, because that company turned into the only practical
default, is the ordinary and predictable cost of over-depending on one vendor's ecosystem, paid mostly by people
who had little say in choosing that dependency to begin with. A strong geomagnetic storm disrupting satellite
links and the ground infrastructure sitting underneath them belongs in that same category rather than in science
fiction, since it's a known, measured risk, which is the entire reason the Kp index gets monitored in the first
place, and something like it will happen again on a timeline that moves entirely on its own schedule. These call
for readiness rather than panic, the same instinct behind triage, sorting what has to keep working from what can
wait before the failure even arrives rather than after it: know which function keeps the patient alive and which one can
wait its turn, and let the search for a
perfect fix come after the one that keeps things breathing in the meantime. Both point toward the same design
rule, the principle that a day's writing
should stand on its own footing rather than depend on the grid staying up or one company staying in business, and
I hold that bar as a thoroughly reasonable one to set. And so it should count as ordinary, the same way a
building keeps a stairwell around even on the days the elevator works fine.

## Every crossing the page owes, and the four nature skips
Matter gives four states and eight named crossings, and the naming runs out exactly where matter stops going
directly. Solid becomes liquid by melting and comes back by freezing, liquid becomes gas by vaporizing and comes
back by condensing, solid reaches gas by subliming and returns by deposition, gas reaches plasma by ionizing and
returns by recombining. Those eight cover every crossing nature performs on its own, and so they arrive with
names already attached.

Writing gets twelve. Ink, type, mark and voice each address each other directly, which means a page performs four
crossings that matter leaves unnamed, and that missing vocabulary feels like the most honest signal available
about how little of this ground anyone has walked yet. The four states, in the order the analogy assigns them:

- **Ink, the solid.** Stroke geometry, pencil, a mark fixed to a position on a surface.
- **Type, the liquid.** The markdown body, flowing, editable, indifferent to where on the page it sits.
- **Mark, the gas.** Highlight, selection, margin note, a layer diffuse across a surface and anchored to ranges.
- **Voice, the plasma.** Speech, audio, prosody, timing, the highest-energy state and the one anchored to a
  moment rather than to a place.

And the twelve crossings, each paired with its inverse, since a crossing that runs one direction only amounts to
a conversion rather than a phase change:

- **Melting, ink to type.** Handwriting becomes editable text. This one runs today, through Apple's on-device
  Vision recogniser in `shared/inkocr.swift`, in English, French and Vietnamese. It behaves as a search index
  though, the recognised text filed beside the strokes so that ⌘F reaches them, which leaves the body of the
  page untouched and the crossing one-way.
- **Freezing, type to ink.** A typed passage becomes a surface a pencil marks in place, strokes anchored to the
  words underneath. Unbuilt, and the anchoring arithmetic for exactly this is where BUG-07 lives.
- **Vaporizing, type to mark.** Typed text becomes range-addressable, highlightable, commentable. Partly
  standing, in the editor's highlight machinery.
- **Condensing, mark to type.** A highlight collapses into a quoted block, a margin note settles into the
  sentence it was about. Unbuilt.
- **Subliming, ink to mark.** A circled region becomes a region-anchored comment directly, skipping recognition
  entirely, which matters most for the handwriting a recogniser declines to read. Unbuilt.
- **Deposition, mark to ink.** A comment sets down as a stroke placed by hand on the page. Unbuilt.
- **Ionizing, mark to voice.** A marked passage reads aloud, or a spoken aside attaches itself to a range.
  Partly standing.
- **Recombining, voice to mark.** A spoken aside lands as a timestamped margin note rather than as body text.
  Unbuilt, and this is the one Apple's live transcript surface looks closest to having already solved.
- **Type to voice, unnamed.** Text read aloud. Running today in `shared/draftpadlibrary.swift`, where
  `willSpeakRange` keeps the spoken word and the written word pointing at each other.
- **Voice to type, unnamed.** Speech becomes editable text. Running today in `shared/speechcore.swift`, Apple's
  SpeechAnalyzer for the languages the system carries and the open ears for everything else.
- **Ink to voice, unnamed.** Handwriting read aloud with the typed intermediate skipped. Unbuilt.
- **Voice to ink, unnamed.** A spoken phrase pinned to a place on the page rather than to a moment in a
  timeline. Unbuilt, and the hardest of the twelve to even picture, which tends to mean it rewards the
  picturing most.

Four states also permit crossings that change the matter while leaving the state alone, the way heating water
leaves it water. Translation, summary and rephrasing all sit there, and they run today across the transcription
surface, each one adding a form linked back to the original under this repo's rule that the owner's words get
transformed rather than replaced.

Two conditions sit above any single crossing, and they describe the actual target better than the crossings do.
Water holds a triple point, 0.01 °C at 611.657 pascals, where ice, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium, all
three genuinely present at once. A page at its triple point carries ink, type, mark and voice simultaneously,
each reachable from any other, and the question of which one counts as the real document dissolves for lack of a
reason to ask it. Water also holds a critical point, 373.95 °C at 22.064 megapascals, above which liquid and gas
stop being distinguishable at all and become one supercritical fluid. A page above its critical point stops
distinguishing what someone said from what they typed, so that one search, one cursor and one edit reach both,
and that strikes me as the more radical of the two targets and the one worth aiming at.

Underneath all of it sits the arithmetic that decides whether any of this stays maintainable. Twelve crossings
built as twelve converters grows as the square of the states, twelve today, twenty at five states, and every
state added multiplies the surface that has to keep working. One canonical core cuts the count to twice the
states, eight today and ten at five, with each state converting to and from a single neutral form, and markdown
already holds that job here by rule. The catch arrives immediately, since markdown carries a body and declines
to carry stroke geometry, range anchors, audio offsets or prosody, which means the canonical core holds the words
while each state keeps a sidecar for whatever markdown drops. That sidecar turns out to be the whole mechanism of
reversibility, and so the question of whether a page comes back from a crossing intact resolves into a storage
decision rather than a conversion one, which feels like the kind of boring, compounding answer that tends to
survive a decade.

## The impossible, treated as merely unbuilt
Here's an exercise I keep coming back to, and I mean it less as daydreaming than as a kind of moral anchor:
assume, on purpose, that fully offline-first, outage-proof, disaster-resilient, multilingual and multimodal
software counts as achievable, and ask how far that assumption would carry this particular project. How far the
current build actually sits from that line stays entirely clear, which keeps this honest rather than delusional. It
reads closer to a refusal to let "that's just how it is" settle in before the edge actually gets tested.

The honest starting point is that what's already planned, a Mac mini sitting quietly at home as the one
always-on peer, is still a single point of failure dressed up as independence. If that machine loses power, or
the house loses power, or the one place it lives is somewhere else entirely, the whole plan collapses back onto
whatever's already cached on whichever device happens to be in hand, and so real disaster resilience would have
to go further than swapping one company's server for one closet's worth of hardware.

This is where the hacker and decentralization communities seem to have already solved more of this than most
writing software has bothered to borrow. BitTorrent's entire premise treats a file as needing many sources
rather than one, since any peer holding any piece of it can serve any other peer that piece, and so the network
grows more resilient the more copies exist. Something built on that same idea, aimed at Recense's own
transcripts and audio instead of movies, would mean a recording and its translation surviving as long as any
single device anywhere still holds a copy, independent of any central server's own subscription staying paid.
Mesh radio protocols like Meshtastic or LoRa, and old-fashioned ham packet radio before them, move small amounts
of data across distances using only their own radio link, independent of any cell tower or Wi-Fi router, which
starts to matter enormously once the question becomes aviation, open water, or a disaster zone where every
ordinary channel goes down at once. And so the oldest trick in the book applies too: letting a file travel
between two people who currently sit out of each other's reach over any network, and trusting whichever device
gains connectivity first to carry it the rest of the way. Call it a sneakernet, regardless of the name anyone
gives it. The project's own offline ladder already builds this in quietly, as its second step.

The unorthodox end of the shelf still holds more. Samizdat, the underground literature that moved through the
Soviet Union one typewritten carbon copy at a time, solved an identical problem a full century before any of
today's protocols existed: how does an idea keep moving when every official channel actively works against it,
rather than simply failing to exist? A steganographer already knows how to hide anything inside anything. A
transcript and its translation could plausibly travel the same way, hidden inside the very audio file carrying
the voice itself, so the record and the recording stay joined even on the one channel that currently earns the
least trust. Onion routing carries a public reputation built almost entirely around its darker uses, yet the
actual principle, where a single relay only ever learns one hop of a message's path rather than both where it
started and where it's headed, solves the same shape of problem whether the threat is a censor or a storm taking
out half the relays in a region. What would it mean to borrow that principle for the second case, and let the
first one keep the headlines? And the devices already doing some of this, pairing a phone to a Mac over
Bluetooth, run on frequency-hopping spread spectrum, a technique built for jam-resistant military radio and
co-patented, in 1942, by the actress Hedy Lamarr alongside the composer George Antheil. The same instinct that
makes a channel hard for an enemy to jam turns out to make a channel hard for a disaster to interrupt too, and
that coincidence deserves more credit than it usually gets.

All of this stays unspecced for now, and some of it may turn out to be the wrong tool for what Recense actually
needs. I'd still rather start from assuming it's possible and seeing how far that takes the design than from
assuming it's impossible and stopping there, and the honest answer to how far that would take us runs further
than anything currently built.

## Where the tuning slips
Some of this is solved well, and some of it is solved the way a system gets solved under deadline, functioning
and documented and quietly wrong in a way I can point to the exact line for. I hold these as boundaries I've
actually managed to locate rather than as complaints, which in my experience counts as most of the work, since a
boundary you can name already stands as a kind of progress over one you can only feel, unable to say where it
starts. What follows reads closer to showing my work at the edge of the map, in case the terrain looks
familiar from where you're standing, or looks different in a way that would teach me something new. And the
assumption above still holds its full reach either way. Finding exactly where an edge sits is what makes it
possible to imagine past it with any real precision, rather than just gesturing at the horizon.

Reduced to one shape, most of what follows turns out to be the same problem wearing nine different costumes: two
things that need to agree on where they stand relative to each other, a clock, a line, a touch, a trust, each
one still waiting on a referee to arrive. One way to put it is a question of standing, whose account of the
facts actually governs when two readings disagree. Another way to put the same gap is a missing source of
truth. Both
descriptions point at the same underlying principle, and once that principle becomes visible, the nine
boundaries below stop reading as nine separate failures and start reading as nine measurements of a single, more
fundamental gap.

- **Two clocks, no referee.** A word Recense hears on the Mac is stored as an offset into the recording, counted
  in seconds since the file began, while the same kind of word, heard on the phone, carries a wall-clock
  timestamp instead, counted in seconds since 1970. The two simply coexist, unreconciled, read by code that
  happens to already know which device wrote which file, which is a relationship held together by memory rather
  than by actual design. How do I reach one shared reference frame with a fix that survives a third writer
  showing up, rather than a one-time script that quietly breaks the moment one does? Whether the honest fix is
  one clock everywhere, with old files migrated on read, or something closer to a vector clock that keeps the
  disagreement visible instead of erasing it the moment two writers stop sharing a frame of reference, stays
  genuinely open for me, and either way, it's a boundary I'd rather map precisely than quietly paper over.

- **Watching a file by asking it, in place of being told.** The Mac app notices edits to Draftpad's Markdown file
  by polling it once a second, alongside four 60 Hz timers and two 20 Hz ones doing other work entirely, with a
  file-watch API such as `NSFilePresenter` or `DispatchSource` still waiting to be wired in. Exactly what asking
  sixty times a second, and receiving an answer on only one of them, costs against simply being told once, stays
  unmeasured by me so far, though I do know it's the kind of inefficiency a laptop's fan tends to notice well
  before a profiler gets asked to look. For something meant to run unattended for hours at a stretch, that's
  really a reliability question more than a performance one, since the whole thing could stop quietly and the
  silence alone would be my only signal, and that silence is a more interesting boundary than the polling itself.

- **Keeping the eye and the ear honest with each other.** The entire premise of lighting a word as it's spoken is
  that sight and sound stay honest with each other about where you are in a sentence, which functions as a
  consistency guarantee more than as a visual effect. A test of Apple's own on-device forced-alignment engine
  against a 200-chapter lecture, using only what's already on the device, every byte of it staying local, anchored 97.5% of words
  correctly, and the longest single stretch where it lost the thread outright ran 3.6 seconds, which is long
  enough for an attentive eye to notice the light had stopped telling the truth. The piece meant to carry this
  live, finding Recense's place in audio it's already heard by acoustic fingerprint alone, meaning hashes rather
  than the audio itself (`shared/fingerprint.swift`, `shared/CaptionSeek.swift`), stands built and tested in
  isolation while still waiting for a wire into live listening. An earlier pass at the harder version of the
  same problem, matching under background noise or playback sped past roughly 1.02×, failed to match far more
  often than it should have. Whether that 3.6-second blind spot and that failure rate share a single cause, or
  stand as two unrelated walls wearing the same symptom, remains genuinely unclear to me, and so does whether
  SpeechAnalyzer already carries a confidence signal capable of seeing a bad stretch coming before the light
  does.

- **Three languages, one seam.** Every string, every voice, every transcript is required to behave identically
  in English, French and Vietnamese, offline, including the ordinary case of a sentence that crosses from one
  language into another mid-breath. Right now, which engine a word belongs to is decided entirely by the
  device's own locale coverage, a property of the hardware rather than of the word itself, and that's a
  distinction worth sitting with for a moment. What happens at the exact instant a sentence changes language
  stays an open question for me: whether that seam already runs invisible, or whether it simply awaits a harder
  push before showing itself, and I'd rather find that particular boundary by testing for it myself than have a
  reader stumble onto it first.

- **A conductor's hand, compressed into one click.** The haptic vocabulary stays deliberately small, at most
  three or four levels per dimension, timed from known word and syllable boundaries rather than from live audio
  analysis, because a vibration arriving a beat late does more damage than silence ever would. On a Mac
  trackpad, that entire vocabulary collapses down to a single click. The swells and the textures only start
  existing once a phone, a watch or a pair of headphones enters the relationship. Whether that's a ceiling built
  into the hardware itself, or one built into an API still waiting for me to find it, remains a genuinely open
  question, and honestly, admitting that stands as a more useful answer than picking one option and sounding
  certain about it.

- **Handoff, built by hand.** Picking up a thought on whichever device happens to be nearest means pairing two
  devices directly, a six-digit code once and pinned keys afterward, with bytes moving over Bonjour and
  peer-to-peer Wi-Fi, clear of any router or account standing in the middle, rather than reaching for whatever
  continuity Apple's own stack already offers two devices signed into the same account. Whether that counted as
  the right call or an unnecessary one stays open to me, and what I'd actually want to know runs narrower than
  that: whether a boring, already-solved primitive sits one layer below Handoff, ready to deliver the same result
  and spare me from reinventing the pairing handshake from first principles, since reinventing a handshake is
  exactly the kind of clever that tends to cost more than it looks like it saves going in.

- **A tap that becomes a drawing.** On a fresh Draftpad page, the very first Apple Pencil touch has to resolve,
  at the instant it lands, whether it's a stroke or a cursor placement, and at touchdown, every available signal
  still falls short of answering that question, so the code decides retroactively, by how far the touch
  travelled (`editor/src/ink.js`: under roughly 3 px counts as a tap, past that counts as a mark). It works. It
  is also a guess wearing a rule's clothing, and I'd like to be precise about that distinction rather than let
  the fix quietly pass as something fully solved. Whether Pencil's own hover distance, azimuth or contact ellipse
  already carries a cleaner, earlier answer to whether a touch was a tap, reachable only from inside a native
  canvas rather than a web one, or whether every app making this exact guess simply lives with the same
  unglamorous norm, stays an open question for me.

- **Ink that remembers a line number, in place of the line.** A stroke anchors to a line index and gets
  re-snapped to `lineIndex × lineHeight` every time the page reflows. For a long stretch that constant was simply
  wrong, 28px, hard-coded, while Draftpad's actual text sits at 18px, and it's fixed now to read the editor's
  real line height at the moment a stroke gets drawn. What's still open, deliberately rather than by oversight,
  runs narrower and more interesting than that original bug: a pure font-size change, with the text itself
  untouched, reflows the words while the ink sitting on top of them stays exactly where it was originally drawn.
  Only a change in a line's position triggered by an actual edit rebuilds that ink correctly today. Whether
  TextKit's or PencilKit's own text-anchoring already treats ink riding on top of moving text as a solved
  relationship stays open for me, given that a canvas simply laid over a CodeMirror view starts from zero on
  that front, independent of whatever TextKit or PencilKit already solved.

- **A gesture built on a framework Apple leaves undocumented.** The three-finger trackpad gestures, layers and
  panel paging, go through the private `MultitouchSupport` framework, which the code's own comments call
  experimental, and that word is an honest one. It works today, which is a different claim from saying it will
  keep working tomorrow. Whether a public surface exists now, or ever existed, for this exact gesture vocabulary
  on a Mac trackpad, or whether private counts as the only address this kind of thing has ever had, stays an
  open question for me, and I'd genuinely like to settle which one is true rather than quietly assume the more
  comfortable answer.

Nine boundaries, nine different shapes, and underneath them, still the same missing referee. The shape repeats
because the principle repeats, and a system that eventually hands every one of these its own referee solves far
more than nine individual bugs. All of them count as exactly the kind of edge I'd rather spend an afternoon
mapping than quietly smooth over.

## The instrument still waiting for me to try
Everything above is about where my own code struggles against itself. There's a separate, more interesting
question underneath all of it, one I keep putting off answering properly, which is how much of this is already
sitting inside Apple's own stack, solved, waiting to be pointed at rather than rebuilt from a blank file. I mean
that literally, as an actual audit still waiting on my own calendar, rather than as a show of modesty. Reduced
to one question, the five below are all really asking the same thing from five different angles: where the
boundary actually sits between what's worth borrowing from a platform and what's worth building by hand, and
which side of that boundary each piece of Recense currently falls on. Treating that boundary as the whole game
is the resourceful move, since the fastest road to something durable usually runs straight through what a much
larger team already solved.

- **System dictation as a universal microphone.** Recense builds its own path from speech to text, engine by
  engine, language by language, essentially from scratch each time. Apple's own dictation already sits
  underneath every text field on every device, system-wide, quietly solving some version of the same problem
  millions of times a day. How much of "hear anything, anywhere, turn it into text" already sits handled at that
  layer, simply waiting to be pointed at, versus how much of it stays deliberately sealed inside the keyboard,
  meant only for that one surface, remains a genuine unknown to me, and answering it would take more than reading
  documentation.

- **A transcript that already exists, elsewhere.** Live Captions, and whatever Apple now calls a call's or a
  recording's own transcript, already produce a timed, on-device text of speech somewhere in the system, and
  that may already be sitting right next to the audio Recense is independently re-transcribing from scratch. I
  keep building a parallel version of the same thing, words, timing, a surface to write on top of them, and
  whether a transcript already sits there, ready to annotate rather than re-derive, remains the real unknown.
  That gap in my own knowledge stands as the actual blocker here, well ahead of anything to do with engineering.

- **Dictation as something searchable, well beyond typed.** The preface's actual complaint is that a teaching
  spoken in a language the machines barely know stays sealed inside its own recording, while an English lecture
  becomes searchable text within minutes. If dictation's output were paired with its audio and indexed the way
  Spotlight indexes nearly everything else on the device, that gap might already be narrower than I've been
  assuming it is. How close that already runs, and how far "searchable" still sits from "findable only inside
  the one app that happened to make it," stays genuinely uncertain to me, and I'd rather name that as an open
  measurement than quote a number I have yet to actually check.

- **Siri as a second way in.** Everything Recense holds, audio, transcript, translation, who said what and when,
  is exactly the kind of corpus a search that understands meaning, rather than one that only matches letters,
  should be good at. Whether that's a door Siri or the rest of Apple Intelligence already opens onto a
  third-party app's own data, or one that stays firmly shut on the other side of the glass, remains open to me.

- **Letting Siri drive, well beyond finding.** The standing rule holds that every action gets a voice command
  alongside a gesture and a menu item, which stays unfinished today, and it's genuinely open whether the right
  owner of that rule is Recense's own voice parser or App Intents, with Siri simply carrying the action the rest
  of the way. Sitting next to that is an older, unbuilt idea for one voice surface that handles dictation,
  editing and agent-like action together instead of three separate ones, plus a separate thought about a small
  local model that answers questions grounded only in what's actually been written or heard, with everything
  else left uninvented. Whether Siri, or whatever now sits behind it on-device, runs close enough to either idea
  to be worth building on, or whether both still await being built completely from scratch, stays open to me, as
  two live questions I'd rather hold apart than collapse into one guess.

All of this reads as an honest map instead of a request, five places where my own knowledge runs out and someone
else's presumably starts, the five I'd open with given an hour with someone who actually knows the inside of
these frameworks, rather than someone, like me, reasoning about them from the outside. Each answer here would
feed straight back into the boundaries above, since knowing what Apple's stack already carries changes which of
those nine problems actually belong to Recense to solve at all. That's the circular part worth sitting with,
since borrowing more from the platform could shrink the list above before a single new line of code gets
written.

## A state still waiting for its name
Everything above assumes the matter only becomes text, audio or ink so far. There's a newer thought underneath
that, barely past being a wish, about letting it also become a small program, something short enough to write in
an afternoon, that runs against one page and does exactly one thing to it: pulls every citation out, turns a
lecture into flashcards, reads a table back in a different order. Draftpad already stands as proof that a small
program can live inside Recense while staying entirely its own thing, a page of its own code, hosted by a native
shell, on the Mac and on the phone both. The open question is whether that relationship generalizes, into many
small, sandboxed things instead of one, written once and carried everywhere the way the player already is,
distributed entirely store-free, cheap enough that having ten of them costs about the same as having a single
one. My own sitting with it so far stays too brief to know whether that's a WidgetKit-shaped problem, an
App-Intents-shaped one, or something closer to Draftpad's own pattern, repeated smaller and many times over.
It's new enough that a folder for it still waits to be created, under this repo's own rule that everything
eventually earns one, and that either means the idea stays short of real yet, or that it marks the next boundary
waiting to be drawn.

## A window borrowed, still unbuilt
A second thought, just as unspecced, sits beside the one above, about a browser living inside Recense rather
than as a separate app, with a model reading alongside whoever's reading, skinned to feel like the one browser
every device already has rather than like something new to learn. The actual engineering question is older and
smaller than the AI framing makes it sound, and it comes down to whether to borrow Safari's own view wholesale,
its engine, its autofill, its shared state with the system browser, someone else's chrome worn as your own, or
to build the surface from a plain web view the way Draftpad already does and only dress it afterward to look
familiar. The AI part raises the genuinely newer question, since a model reading a page aloud, or answering a
question about it, performs exactly the kind of act Recense already insists on sourcing, naming where every
answer came from as a condition of performing it at all, so whatever this becomes has to hold a live, constantly
shifting page to the same standard a stored, finished transcript already meets, even while the ground underneath
it keeps moving. Underneath both questions sits a third still awaiting resolution from me: browsing stands as
the one thing on this entire list unlikely to be offline-first by nature in the first place, and whether that
marks it as the wrong shape for Recense altogether, or simply the one part of it allowed to need the network the
way a link someone else sent already does, stays open.

This one's barely a sentence in the backlog right now, and it's been sitting there longer than the one above,
which tells me something about which of the two I actually believe in more, a preference I've left unnamed so
far.

## The room this needs
One decision is already made, and only the sizing is still owed. Cloudflare's Worker and R2 are meant to retire
into a Mac mini, an always-on third peer sitting quietly at home, doing what a cloud used to do, owned outright
this time instead of rented. Moving the dependency off Cloudflare relocates it rather than removing it, onto a
different single point, this Mac's own power and this Mac's own drive, and that's worth naming honestly, the
principle being that a move counts as progress only when it's named for what it actually changes. This stays
concrete to me rather than abstract: I've already run a piece of this, the fallback transcription mostly, on the
machine I'm writing this from, and watched memory pressure turn a job that should take a minute into one that
took ten. The disk underneath it carries the matching problem, since bulk folders already need to move off this
Mac's internal drive just to make room for anything else to happen at all. My interest in relocating either
mistake onto a second machine, and calling that progress, stays at zero. I want to actually size this one, once,
correctly, the way a thing only has to get done right the first time when the goal is doing it exactly once.
What it would have to carry, all at once, under one roof, is still an open sum:

- A sync relay and ledger, awake nearly the entire day, cheap enough in power and heat that turning it off is
  rarely the easier option.
- The Omnilingual ASR fallback mentioned above, a real model rather than a Mac's worth of SpeechAnalyzer, for
  whichever languages still await Apple's own engine reaching them.
- Kokoro, rendering a chaptered audiobook (`FEAT-01`), occasional and bursty work that's probably fine on
  anything.
- Possibly, later, a small local model doing retrieval over my own writing for a source-grounded assistant
  (noted in the backlog only as "the model on the SanDisk," still awaiting both its build and its sizing).
- And the watching itself remains unbuilt: a health check, an alert, a second machine standing by to notice if
  the first one goes quiet, each one still only an idea on paper, the equivalent of a patient still missing a
  vital-signs monitor.

What stays genuinely uncertain to me, stated plainly rather than hedged: whether unified memory or GPU core count
marks the actual bottleneck once two or three of those run concurrently instead of one at a time. Whether a base
M4 with more RAM quietly beats an M4 Pro with less for this particular mix of workloads stays just as open. And
how large a personal, years-deep archive genuinely gets remains the number that decides whether the internal SSD
suffices or this needs to be designed around external storage from day one. If a figure in that list already runs wrong, I would
rather hear it before the thing gets bought than after it's sitting on a desk being wrong expensively, and I've
already paid once for guessing, an instructive enough tuition on its own.

## Privacy
Client matters never enter this repo. Secrets stay out of git: `.dev.vars`, `rclone.conf`, `.env`, wrangler's
`.wrangler/` cache and the iOS device token are ignored.

---

Every question above stands as a genuine one, and the whole project amounts to a vote of confidence in Apple's
frameworks rather than a complaint about them, since betting the whole system on them, deliberately, counts as
the actual point, a risk I'm naming out loud rather than quietly hoping goes unnoticed. What I keep coming back
to is the principle that almost everything above is really one question asked nine or ten different ways, which
is where this system's actual edge sits, and whether that edge exists because the problem runs genuinely hard, or
because my own looking simply stopped short of hard enough. Those two kinds of edge look identical from the
inside, at least to me. Pulled apart, the habits running through all of this read as an insistence on evidence
before any conclusion gets drawn, an instinct for finding exactly where the one true point of failure hides, a
sense for which problem earns attention first, and a plain resourcefulness that treats a closed door as one
whose hinges still await a try. Finding any of this already solved somewhere out there would please me a great deal, and
leaving it unsolved everywhere would still leave me preferring a precisely mapped boundary over one smoothed over
and quietly called finished. That's a large part of why this stays public, open equally to me and to everyone
else reading it.

## Still looking for the next one to try
Read all together, this amounts to a running list of unconventional techniques I've picked up, turned over, and

browser wearing someone else's chrome, a program small enough to fit in an afternoon. The question underneath
all of it stays genuinely mine to ask, and I'd actually like an answer: which of these have you already tried
yourself, and which ones remind you of something you built once, or modified until it did something its own
instructions stayed entirely silent about? Is there an approach missing from this particular list, one you've
wanted to try for years, waiting only on its occasion to arrive, or one that still awaits a first attempt
anywhere, visible only to someone bold enough to walk straight past the conventional answer and reach for the
untried one instead? Would you add one here, keep one, set one aside, or rework one until it fit differently,
the kind of daring, untraditional problem-solving that's willing to take an existing approach apart completely
and build something its own instructions left entirely unauthorized? This amounts to an open invitation to keep trying
