# Preface

## I. Before the First Sound

Every performance begins before the first sound. The one who leads an ensemble raises a hand, and in that suspended instant the players breathe together and the room grows attentive. Sound then enters a silence that has been prepared for it.

I have come to believe that verbal exchange begins in the same way. Before two people can understand each other, they must first agree to listen. Recense was born of a conversation about conversation itself, held with someone who teaches it as a craft. We were drawn together by a shared regard for speech and for the thought that lives within it, and we agreed that speaking with another person is a skill worth studying. It was there that I first saw how much of speaking is in truth an act of listening.

Our talk turned to how such a skill is taught, and that question opened another. Verbal exchange and improvisation suffer from the same misunderstanding. Both are imagined as pure spontaneity, a gift that settles on the fortunate at birth. It is simply assumed that a person learns them as they go, and so both are left to chance. The misunderstanding is convenient, since it spares us the work of teaching. I began to suspect that two practices burdened with the same misunderstanding might share the same nature.

## II. Composing in Time

To improvise is to compose and perform in a single act. The piece comes into being at the moment it is heard, and each phrase answers the one that came before.

Impromptu speech answers to this description exactly. A question is asked and the room waits, and a person shapes a reply in the very act of giving it. Impromptu speech is therefore improvisation. A verbal exchange is a succession of such replies, each formed from what the previous voice offered, and so every exchange is impromptu from its first word to its last. It follows that verbal exchange is improvisation. In principle the two are one practice. In practice they have been given separate names and separate teachers, and they are studied in separate buildings.

Placed side by side, each sheds light on the other. Improvisers are trained to receive what is offered and to build upon it, and that discipline serves anyone who must answer a question with little time to prepare. Improvisers also rely on form. A jazz pianist invents over a progression of chords that the whole band holds in common. A freestyle rapper builds verse upon verse over a beat that keeps its steady course. Freedom of this kind rests upon structure, much as a melody rests upon the harmony beneath it.

Impromptu speech possesses structures of its own, and the orator's craft has kept them across centuries. One is the arrangement of ideas in threes. A blues verse states its line, states it again and then turns on the third, and a listener hears three points as a completed thought for much the same reason. Another is the practice of naming a thought's destination before setting out toward it. A band follows a singer who calls the change a bar ahead, and a listener follows a speaker who announces where the argument will arrive. Such structures allow an improvisation to endure long enough to reach its meaning. It was at this meeting of two disciplines that I first believed Recense could exist.

## III. The Measure of a Gift

Ease is something we observe and distance from observation to cause is greater than it appears. I was the first in my family to take up an instrument. Even so my ear and my hands matured early, ahead of my peers.

A player who has lost their sight comes to a piece through the ear and the hand, while a sighted player comes to it partly through the page, and each develops strengths the other may lack. A quiet passage in a lullaby and a quiet passage in a stadium anthem ask for different sounds, and talent is equally bound to what it is asked to serve. There are countless ways to measure a person, and convention chooses only a few. Every person carries a gift that some measure would reveal, and convention decides which gifts are heard.

## IV. The Practice Room

The discovery that improvisation can be taught by method turned my attention to the learner, and in time to myself. What is it that makes something new easier to grasp? I let the less orthodox side of my character answer. It is the side that receives an obstacle as an invitation and a rule as a suggestion. It grows braver as the difficulty rises, and it has a gift for finding another way through. Delight has its part in this as well, though delight deserves an essay of its own.

The practice room is where that side of character does its truest work. A difficult passage arrives with fingerings printed by an editor, and when they fail me I invent others. I move a breath to a different place in the phrase. I learn the passage backward from its final note. I try a stranger fingering and hear the line begin to sing. In time the challenge itself becomes the game.

The question was whether this attitude could leave the practice room and become a way of living. Verbal exchange suggested that it could. The habit that had carried me through every unfamiliar subject might be written down as code. The craft of shaping sound in time could then serve as a tuning fork for interpretation. It could help a person hear how ideas are offered and received in conversation, and it could help them approach a language that is new to the ear. Recense rests on that challenge, and it is my attempt to meet it.

## V. A Score for the Verbal World

A transcript resembles a page that carries only the tune and its chords. The words are present, and the phrasing is left to whoever reads them.

Verbal exchange has its hidden mechanics, and they recede further as soon as a second voice enters. Text preserves the words and lets everything around them fall away. The pause before an answer disappears, along with the voice that rises as conviction gathers and the softening that invites a reply. These things can be recovered. Meaning leaves its traces in what is seen and in what is heard, and patient attention can gather them.

Those who write sound down for others to perform met this difficulty long ago, and the score is their answer. A score is multimodal, reaching the mind through several senses at once. The eye reads it and the inner ear hears it, and the hands and breath of the players carry it into the room. Beside a score, text is flat. A score has depth, since it sets pitch above time and intention above both. A few signs at the opening of a page establish the home key and the meter. Words in the margin ask for tenderness or urgency. From these a gathering of strangers can arrive at a shared understanding of what one composer felt. What remains hidden in live speech is precisely what such markings bring to light. A gradual swelling of sound records conviction gathering. A note held beyond its written length records a speaker holding the room.

A score is also a conversation carried across time. It passes between the one who wrote it and every person involved in its interpretation, from the player at the back of the section to the one who leads the ensemble.

The one who leads a symphony shapes the sound in silence, and that work begins before the first note. A single lift of the hands establishes the tempo and character of what follows, and the ensemble breathes with it. In speech the same work is done by an intake of breath or a phrase that announces a turn. The same hands keep the balance. A lowered palm softens the loudest voices so that a quieter one may come through. Each of these gestures signals intention before a sound is made. A rest in conversation does the same work. A deliberate pause tells the listener what the speaker means to do next, whether to let a point settle or to offer the turn to someone else.

Imagine, then, how a score written for verbal exchange might even help someone learn the lullaby a grandparent sings in the language of their childhood, and sing it back to them! Its upper line would carry what is said and its lower line how it is said, and the two would be read as one. A learner could see where a speaker leans and where they yield, and could learn to speak with the same care. Such a score would teach articulation in the performer's sense, the shaping of each sound so that it carries. It would teach as well the intention held within a rest.

This is why multimodal learning stands at the center of Recense. Speech reaches us through the ear, yet it is shaped by the face and the breath, and it is understood most fully when more of those channels are open. Sumby and Pollack showed in 1954 that seeing a speaker's face makes speech in noise far easier to follow. When the lips show one syllable while the ear hears another, the mind often perceives a third, so what the eye sees can alter what the ear hears. Training that engages several senses tends to produce faster and more effective learning than training through one. Words spoken aloud are remembered better than words read in silence. Subtitles in the language being sung have helped television audiences in India improve their reading. The findings converge on a single conclusion. The senses learn best in concert.

This is how we learn to play. A piece entered through our eyes and our ears and come to rest at last in our hands. Recense offers the same passage into a language. The ear follows the voice while the eye follows the light across each word, and our voice joins them.

## VI. A Shared Tuning

For centuries instruments were tuned to favor certain keys. Chords in those keys sounded pure, and chords in distant keys sounded harsh. A tuning that divides the octave into twelve equal steps changed this. It made every key available and allowed any instrument to play with any other. The price was purity. Every interval except the octave was adjusted slightly, and the particular color of each key was smoothed away.

English has become that shared tuning for the world's ideas. It allows people of many traditions to play together, and a great deal of knowledge now travels through it. The convenience is real, and so is the cost. Every language holds colors and intervals of meaning that soften when everything must pass through a single tuning.

Fluency in English is distributed unevenly, and the reasons are political before they are linguistic. The world is plural, governed by many states, and each state decides what its children will study. Curricula follow history and alliance and budget, and a child's second language is usually chosen long before the child has any voice in the matter. Some grow up thinking in English. Others grow up in languages that English rarely visits, among them the languages of countries under sanction, held apart from the wider conversation. Some of these languages hold indigenous knowledge that could benefit the West. Some of the West's ideas, in turn, could serve developing nations that the world's attention passes by.

Much of the world's wisdom is spoken. It lives in voices long before it reaches a page, and it reaches the page through transcription. Transcription has its limits. The machines that turn speech into text learned mostly from English and a handful of powerful languages, and their development has followed region and privilege. A lecture in English becomes searchable text within minutes. A teaching spoken in a language the machines barely know may remain sealed within its recording.

And so the spoken wisdom of English and the spoken wisdom of other languages face each other through glass. Each can make out the other in outline, a title or a face upon a screen, while the words themselves stay muffled behind the pane. Ideas that might answer one another wait on either side, unheard. Access to ideas, I have come to think, is a question of freedom as much as of fluency.

Anyone who has accompanied a singer knows that two parties with different instincts can meet. The singer stretches a line and the accompanist waits for it. The accompanist holds the pulse and the singer leans against it. The phrase comes alive in that negotiation.

Recense is my attempt to open that window. It brings a voice from any language to the listener who goes in search of it, word by word and in time, with the original and the English sounding together. The English supplies the common tuning, and the original keeps its color. What I hope for is simple to state and difficult to achieve. A thought first spoken in a small corner of the world should be able to reach someone far away who is searching for it. There it may settle into the reflexes as a practiced passage does, until it shapes a life lived in any language.

## Coda

Every performance ends as it began, in silence. The one who leads the ensemble holds the final gesture a moment after the sound has faded, and the room listens to what remains. I am grateful for the conversation from which all of this began, and for the person who shared it with me. That gratitude belongs in the silence, where it can be heard most clearly.
